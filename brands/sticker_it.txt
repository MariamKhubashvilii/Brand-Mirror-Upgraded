## 1. Sticker it — brand snapshot

**Who we are.** Sticker it is a UK-based (Bristol) custom sticker, label, magnet, and badge printing company, launched in 2018. It's the sister brand of Kartdavid, founded in 2007 by brothers Michael and David Rubie-Todd to make custom graphics for karting/motorsport — Sticker it grew out of that materials and colour expertise once the team started fielding requests from businesses outside motorsport.

**What we sell.** Core product lines: **stickers, labels, magnets, badges, sticker packs, blank labels, samples.** Within stickers specifically, three ways the site segments the range (this is the natural menu for blog topic ideas and internal links — see Section 2):
- **By shape:** die cut, kiss cut, rounded corner, circle, rectangle, oval, square, sticker sheets
- **By material/effect:** white vinyl, clear, holographic, glitter, mirror silver, mirror gold, fluorescent, gold foil, biodegradable paper, eco-friendly (white/clear/holo/glitter/gold/silver), transfer
- **By use case:** heavy duty, front adhesive, static cling, floor, wall, eggshell (anti-tamper)

**Who we sell to.** Two audiences on every page, never picking one over the other: **brands and small businesses** (packaging, marketing, bulk/consistent orders — proof points include Michelin, BBC, Spotify, Netflix, Coca-Cola, Nike) and **individual creators and hobbyists** (Etsy sellers, artists, LEGO builders, van-lifers — signalled by the £1 sample pack and "no minimum order").

**Value props to reach for when a blog post needs a natural product mention:** free unlimited proofs before printing, UK-made with 4-day turnaround, free shipping, no minimum order (1 to 10,000+), 100% money-back guarantee, eco-friendly/compostable options.

**Numbers worth having on hand:** 27 million+ stickers/products printed, 18,000+ customers, £1.2 million+ invested in manufacturing, printed on an HP Indigo digital press plus an in-house laser cutter, based at Filwood Green Business Park (solar-powered, BREEAM 93%+ certified).

## 3. What the blog is for

The blog exists to catch search traffic a product page can't rank for, then funnel readers back to the shop through in-body links (see Section 2). Nearly every post is a direct answer to a real, specific question — not brand storytelling. The single highest-traffic post on the whole site (tens of thousands of monthly visits) is a practical how-to: getting stickers off glass. That tells you what this blog is actually for: solving a concrete problem, reliably, at scale.

## 4. Two axes shape every post — category and format

This is the most important structural fact in this file. **Category** (Product / Technology / Tutorials / News) governs *register* — how formal, how personal, how much first-person the post allows. **Format** (How-to / Listicle / Guide / Comparison / Definition / Story / Roundup — the site's own internal page-type labels) governs *structure* — how the post is organised, how it opens, how it closes. A post is always both: "News category, How-to format" or "Product category, Definition format." Drafting should name both before writing a word.

### The category axis (register)

| | Product | Technology | Tutorials | News |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Job | Buying-decision content, links hard into shop pages | Deeper technical pieces for sophisticated buyers/press | How-to for people who bought, or are DIYing | Catch-all; busiest category; most personal content lives here |
| Person | You / your | Neutral third person, occasional "you" | You (direct instruction) | I / you — first person shows up here |
| Jargon | Defined in-line | Introduced and explained (CMYK, colour gamut) | Minimal, task-focused | Minimal |
| First-person / anecdote | Rare | Absent | Rare | Present — the one place the voice loosens into personal register |

### The format axis (structure)

These are the site's own page-type tags, confirmed against real posts:

**How-to** — By far the most common and highest-performing format. Opens with a short TL;DR or "quick rundown by surface/situation" before the full steps. Frequently organised as a lookup table dressed in prose: "Glass: try X. Plastic: try Y. Wood: try Z." Numbered or clearly segmented methods follow, each 3-5 short steps. Often closes on a practical caution ("test on a hidden area first") rather than a hard product push. Examples: *How to clean stickers off glass* (the site's #1 traffic page), *Sticker residue won't budge? Try these 8 fixes*, *How to remove stickers from a car*, *How to remove sticker residue from clothes*, *How to apply window decals without bubbles*, *How to make custom LEGO stickers*.

**Listicle** — A grouped set of short items, each self-contained in 1-3 sentences, no item requiring the previous one. Voice inside each item is punchier and more clipped than narrative prose. Examples: *13 sticker types (and where to use them)*, *What to do with stickers*, *Sticker storage ideas*, *Displaying stickers*.

**Guide** — Reference content organised for scanning and lookup rather than reading start to finish: charts, size tables, an embedded FAQ block near the end matching real search queries almost verbatim. Less personality than How-to, more like a spec sheet with connective sentences. Examples: *A cheat sheet for sticker sizes (by shape & use)*, *What is the standard bumper sticker size?*, *Understanding digital printing*.

**Comparison** — Two sub-modes worth telling apart:
- *Third-party comparison* (no product tie-in): recommends outside brands/tools on their merits, e.g. *What are the best printers for stickers?* recommends specific Epson and Canon models with a comparison table. This is pure authority-building — it doesn't sell Sticker it's own range at all, because it isn't about sticker materials.
- *Material comparison* (on-brand): weighs Sticker it's own material options against each other using the same "use X when Y" imperative structure, e.g. *What are stickers made of?* ("Pick BOPP for products exposed to oil, water, or cold... Use polyester or PET for industrial or safety use...").
Both sub-modes commit to real recommendations rather than hedging.

**Definition** — Short, reassuring, often opens with a rhetorical question and an explicit promise that the explanation won't be jargon-heavy. Leans on a plain-language analogy to land the concept. From *What is a die cut sticker?*: the post frames the explanation as "jargon-free and easy to understand" before comparing a cutting die to a cookie cutter. Examples: *What is a die cut sticker?*, *What is a kiss cut sticker?*, *Decals vs stickers* (opens on an empathetic, relatable admission rather than a rhetorical question — "if you've ever ordered one when you really needed the other, you're not alone" — a second valid Definition-format opener).

**Story** — The one format with a genuinely different skeleton: chronological, era-by-era H2 sections (ancient Egypt → 19th-century postage stamps → 1930s self-adhesive labels → 1970s-80s sticker craze → today), each grounded in a real date or named inventor rather than a vague "long ago." Closes with an explicit, clearly-labelled **"About Sticker it"** section — a distinct closer this format alone uses; other formats never break the fourth wall this directly. Example: *The history of stickers*.

**Roundup** — Closely related to Listicle but lighter and longer: dozens of very short entries (a phrase or single sentence each), high whimsy, minimal explanation per item, meant for skimming and inspiration rather than decision-making. Example: *50 cool sticker ideas for 2026* ("Add charm to planners with sunny smiles and rainy-day faces... Create candy-coloured cupcakes, donuts, and macarons with cute expressions").

### Why this matters for drafting
Format can override the category default. A News-category post in Story format (*history of stickers*) still closes with a branded "About Sticker it" section even though News posts otherwise favour a soft, understated link. A Product-category post in Comparison format about third-party printers skips the sales close entirely, because there's genuinely nothing of Sticker it's own to link to. Pick format first — it decides the skeleton — then let category tune the voice inside it.

## 5. Core voice pillars (hold across every category and format)

1. **Answer first, sell second.** The opening line resolves the reader's actual question. Product mentions arrive last, usually one soft link.
2. **Numbers over adjectives.** Even in casual posts: fluorescent ink reflects roughly 300% of light vs 90% for standard colour; biodegradable material composts in about 12 weeks; a minimum detail size of 3mm for transfer stickers.
3. **Honest trade-offs, stated plainly.** Eco-friendly clear material isn't dishwasher-safe; transfer stickers cost more because someone hand-weeds them; window clings lose grip in rain. Candour is a deliberate trust signal, not a flaw to write around.
4. **Second person throughout**, even in Technology and Guide posts.
5. **Short sentences, short paragraphs.** Often one sentence per paragraph for a key claim or transition.
6. **Contractions are normal.** "Don't," "doesn't," "it's," "you'll," "you're not alone."
7. **The TL;DR-before-detail pattern is the default for practical content, not an occasional trick.** Every top-traffic How-to post pulled for this guide opens with some form of quick summary — a numbered list, a by-surface breakdown, or a one-paragraph "short answer" — before the full explanation. Treat this as close to mandatory for new How-to posts, not optional.

## 6. Opener patterns (pick one deliberately)

- **Concrete, specific observation** (not generic intro): the LEGO post opens by pointing out that a lot of "official" LEGO detail is actually printed stickers, not moulded bricks.
- **Answers the title question immediately**: *Are window clings reusable?* opens "Yes, most window clings are reusable" in its first sentence, then explains why.
- **Rhetorical question + reassurance of simplicity** (Definition format): "But what exactly does it mean?... We're here to help you. This short guide tells you exactly what a die-cut sticker really is — jargon-free and easy to understand."
- **Empathetic, relatable admission** (Definition/Comparison format): "If you've ever ordered one when you really needed the other, you're not alone."
- **Quick-rundown-by-situation** (How-to format): naming the 2-3 variables that actually matter ("bad prep, wrong placement, peeling too fast") before any steps begin.

## 7. Closer patterns

- **Default: soft, low-pressure product link.** One sentence, one link, no hard sell.
- **Story format only: explicit "About Sticker it" branded section.** Named as its own subheading, not blended into the narrative.
- **Comparison (third-party) format: no product close at all**, or a very indirect one — the post's job was to be genuinely useful, not to sell.
- **How-to format: sometimes a caution rather than a close** ("test on a hidden area first"), prioritising the reader not getting hurt over getting one more link in.

## 8. Vocabulary and phrase bank

**Recurring functional phrases:**
- "Here's how..." / "Here's what to do next..."
- "That said,..." / "In practice,..."
- "The short answer:..." / "The good news is..."
- "Before we get into the full how-tos, here's a quick rundown..."
- "Use X for Y. Try A for B." (imperative, paired recommendation — very common in Comparison and Guide posts)

**Recurring product-boilerplate phrases:**
- "resistant to scratches, water, and fading in sunlight"
- "digitally cut in any shape around your design"
- "medium-strength adhesive"
- "ships in 4 working days" / "free proof before printing"

**Words the copy leans on:** custom, durable, waterproof, resistant, compostable, digitally cut, laminate, adhesive.

**Words/moves to avoid:**
- Unsupported superlatives without a number or comparison behind them.
- Passive corporate phrasing ("it is recommended that").
- Hedging both ways in a comparison — the brand commits to an answer.
- Long, clause-stacked sentences outside Technology/Guide posts.

## 9. Title and meta description patterns

Confirmed formats: "[X] explained," "how to [task]," "[material] vs [material]," "are/is [product] [property]," "the ultimate [topic] checklist," "the 7 best places to [do a thing]," "what is a [product]?," "[number] [things] for [year]" (Roundup/Listicle), "[topic] won't [problem]? Try these [number] fixes" (How-to).

Meta descriptions name the specific question or comparison, name the deciding factors, and imply full resolution in one or two sentences — never a vague teaser.

## 10. Do / Don't quick reference

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with the real question or a concrete observation | Open with "In today's world, stickers are more popular than ever" |
| Give a TL;DR before a long explanation | Make the reader scroll through backstory to find the answer |
| Back claims with a number, spec, or named mechanism | Say "really durable" with nothing behind it |
| Commit to a real recommendation in comparisons | Hedge with "it depends, both are great" |
| Match register to category (loosen in News, tighten in Technology) | Use one flat voice everywhere |
| Link the specific product page (Section 2) when a named product comes up | Link the general /custom-stickers hub as a lazy default |
| Use the format's real closer (soft link / About section / caution / nothing) | Force a product link onto every single post regardless of fit |

## 11. Quick pre-publish checklist

- Have I named both the category (register) and the format (structure) before drafting?
- Does the opening line answer a real question rather than describing the brand first?
- Is every strong claim backed by a number, spec, or named mechanism?
- Is there at least one honest limitation, if one genuinely exists?
- Are paragraphs short — often one or two sentences?
- Have I linked to the specific product page for anything named, per Section 2, rather than just the hub?
- Does the close match the format (not a reflexive product link if the format doesn't call for one)?
- If it's a How-to: is there a scannable summary near the top?
- Does the meta description name the specific question/comparison and imply full resolution?
